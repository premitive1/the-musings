When one patrticipates in bitcoin meetups and events some others might
come to conclude, even without much actual evidence, that you have a lot
of money and there are those in this world who would have your money by
hook or by crook. I've warned everyone enough about crypto investing so
let me turn our attention today to the somewhat more mundane phenomena
of fraud. When properly understood the nature of this scam becomes
superficial and obvious. Typically, a bad actor misrepresents themselves
as someone their victim otherwise trusts. They may appear as someone you
know in real life, a celebrity you admire, or an institution with whom
you do business. In mistaking them for someone you trust, they hope to
either gain access to your accounts, or to have you send them money
directly.

In the first case they may come to you as a friend, asking for a quick
loan to help with a lucrative last minute deal or unforseen expense. In
the second case they may claim to be a successful celebrity investor,
with an exclusive investing opportunity on an as yet unknown web3
trading platform, and if you put your bitcoin in now, you will get far
ahead of the market. In the final case, you may get an email or a phone
call from someone representing themselves as your bank, or other
institution that handles your money, like an online retailer. Maybe
there's something wrong with your account--perhaps they claim a large
transaction was processed that you didn't approve. To resolve the issue
they need you to provide login details or identifiable information about
yourself. In all these cases the fraudster wants you to give them your
money under false pretenses and if you get tricked, there is likely no
recourse for recovering any of your funds.

These are but a smattering of the many styles of fraud and deception
existing in this era of telecommunications and with a little due
diligence you can generally avoid them all. To start with, be cautious
when someone contacts you, especially from a new account or phone
number. If you are contacted directly, but are asked to identify
yourself, this is often a red flag. Rather than give any of your
personal information over, end that communication and find the official
contact information of the person or party in question and connect with
them directly to confirm the reason for the initial contact. It's also
possible that the actual credential of a person has been compromised.
This situation requires extra caution. Before you seriously consider
sending money to a friend in a rush based on a text conversation, ask
for a live video call as an extra step in verifying their identify.

Remember those two recommendations to protect yourself from petty fraud:

1\. Do not volunteer your personal credentials when someone contacts
you. (Personal credentials as in your name, address, contact
information, passwords or private keys)

2\. Verify that you're talking to who you think you are, by either doing
a live video call or meeting in person. (Though soon, with the growth of
AI deepfakes even video calls will no longer be good enough.)
